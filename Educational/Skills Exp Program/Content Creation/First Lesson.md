# First Lesson — Content Creation

> **Welcome!** Content creation is turning what you know into things other people can read, watch, or hear — posts, videos, newsletters, threads. Today you'll learn the beginner's loop (idea → outline → draft → publish), destroy the myth that you need to be "good first," and actually ship one small piece of content before this session ends. Shipping is the skill; everything else is refinement.

---

## 1. Why learn this?

- Every skill in this collection becomes more valuable when you can *explain it* — content is how opportunities find you.
- The creators you admire aren't more talented; they've published more often and learned from each attempt.
- It costs almost nothing to start and compounds: each piece teaches you what your audience wants.

**Where the other lessons meet this one:** [SEO](../SEO/First%20Lesson.md) makes content findable; [product-marketing skills](../product-marketing%20skills/First%20Lesson.md) turns content into sales; [Web Design](../Web%20Design/First%20Lesson.md) makes it pleasant to read.

## 4. Core concepts

### 4.1 The loop: idea → outline → draft → edit → publish
All content — a tweet or a documentary — follows this loop. Beginners spend 90% of their time on the draft and 10% on the rest; experienced creators invert it. **Outline before you draft** — it's the difference between writing toward a destination and wandering.

### 4.2 Ideas come from three places (not from waiting for inspiration)
1. **Problems you solved** — "how I fixed X" is eternally publishable.
2. **Questions people asked you** — if one person asked, a thousand wondered.
3. **Things you changed your mind about** — "I used to think X, now I think Y" is magnetic.

Keep an **idea list** (a note on your phone or a [Notion](../Notion/First%20Lesson.md) database — you know how to build one). Capturing ideas when they appear is 80% of never-running-out.

### 4.3 The outline: promise, points, punch
Every good piece answers three questions:
- **Promise:** what will the reader get? (the title + first line)
- **Points:** the 2–4 ideas that deliver the promise (the body)
- **Punch:** the takeaway or action (the ending)

Write those three lines *before* any prose. If you can't state the promise, you don't have an idea yet — you have a topic.

### 4.4 Draft fast, edit slow
Drafting and editing use different brain modes and sabotage each other. Drafting rule: **don't look back, don't fix sentences, just reach the end** — a bad finished draft beats a perfect paragraph one. Editing is where you cut the first 20% and sharpen verbs. Never do both simultaneously.

### 4.5 Ship before you're ready
The audience forgives rough edges; the algorithm rewards consistency; *you* learn more from one published piece than ten unpublished ones. Perfectionism is just fear with good manners.

## 5. Hands-on exercise

**Step 1 — Build your idea list (10 min).** Create a note titled "Ideas." Using the three sources above, write down **five ideas** right now. Not good ideas — *count* of ideas. Examples to prime you: "3 terminal commands I use daily," "why I switched my notes to one database," "the typing drill that finally made me faster," "what making my first workflow taught me about automation."

**Step 2 — Pick the smallest one (5 min).** Choose the idea you could explain to a friend in 2 minutes. Small and done beats ambitious and abandoned. (Keep the rest — that's next week's content.)

**Step 3 — Write the skeleton (10 min).** For your pick, write three lines:
- **Promise:** "In this post you'll learn ___"
- **Points:** 2–3 bullets
- **Punch:** "So the one thing to do is ___"

If you did today's [Notion First Lesson](../Notion/First%20Lesson.md), this skeleton works beautifully as a database with Promise/Points/Punch properties — one row per future post.

**Step 4 — The 15-minute draft (15 min).** Timer on. Expand each skeleton bullet into 2–4 sentences. **No re-reading, no fixing** — messy is correct. When the timer ends, stop even mid-sentence.

**Step 5 — Edit once (10 min).** Now edit: cut the first sentence (drafts warm up slowly), cut 20% of the words, make the title concrete ("How I..." / "3 ways..." / "Why X beats Y for beginners"). Read it aloud once — your ear catches what your eye forgives.

**Step 6 — Ship it (5 min).** Publish on your chosen platform. If you truly can't publish publicly today, send it to one friend with "I'm practicing writing — honest feedback welcome." That counts as shipped. Note how it felt; the dread is always bigger than the thing.

**Expected result:** five captured ideas, one outline, one edited draft, and one *published* piece of content — a complete loop, executed.

## 6. Cheat sheet

| Item | Rule of thumb |
|---|---|
| Idea sources | problems solved / questions asked / mind-changes |
| Idea list | capture instantly, judge never (while capturing) |
| Outline | Promise → Points → Punch, before prose |
| Drafting | timer on, no looking back, reach the end |
| Editing | after drafting only; cut 20%; read aloud |
| Titles | concrete beats clever: numbers, outcomes, specificity |
| First piece | 150–300 words is a complete post |
| Frequency | weekly for 8 weeks beats daily for 3 days |
| Feedback | ship to one real human before shipping to the algorithm |

---

## 7. Common beginner mistakes

1. **Waiting to be "ready."** Ready is a feeling after publishing, not before. Volume reveals your voice; nobody's first piece is their voice.
2. **Drafting while editing.** Re-reading mid-draft wakes the critic and kills momentum. Separate the modes — timer helps.
3. **No outline.** You'll write 300 words of beautiful nothing. Three skeleton lines first, always.
4. **Starting with your magnum opus.** First pieces are push-ups, not performances. Practice on small ideas.
5. **Publishing into the void once and quitting.** The loop compounds: publish weekly, review monthly, double down on what got responses.

## 8. Check yourself

1. What are the five stages of the creator's loop, and which two should never happen at the same time?
2. Name the three idea sources.
3. What three lines make up the skeleton, and why write them before the draft?
4. Why does the 15-minute draft forbid re-reading?
5. Your draft is finished but feels terrible. What do you do?

<details><summary><strong>Answers</strong></summary>

1. Idea → outline → draft → edit → publish. Drafting and editing must be separated — different brain modes.
2. Problems you solved, questions people asked you, things you changed your mind about.
3. Promise, Points, Punch — because the outline proves the idea is ready and gives the draft a destination.
4. Re-reading wakes the inner editor; the goal is a finished bad draft, not a perfect unfinished one.
5. Edit it (cut the opening, cut 20%, sharpen the title), then ship anyway — the feeling is not a fact.
</details>
