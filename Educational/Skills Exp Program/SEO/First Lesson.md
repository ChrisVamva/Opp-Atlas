# First Lesson — SEO

> **Welcome!** SEO (Search Engine Optimization) is making your pages understandable and attractive to search engines, so the right people find you when they search. Today you'll learn how search actually works, do your first keyword research, and optimize a real page's title and description. No jargon walls, no paid tools — just the fundamentals that everything else builds on.

---

## 1. Why learn this?

- Search traffic is "free" attention: you write it once, Google sends visitors for years.
- It's measurable and fair-ish: small sites *can* win specific searches against big ones — if they target the right searches.
- These fundamentals transfer to YouTube, app stores, and AI assistants (which increasingly cite pages too).

**Where the other lessons meet this one:** [Content Creation](../Content%20Creation/First%20Lesson.md) is what SEO optimizes; [Web Design](../Web%20Design/First%20Lesson.md) affects speed and usability, which search engines measure.

## 4. Core concepts

### 4.1 Search engines do three things
1. **Crawl** — robots follow links, discovering pages.
2. **Index** — each page is stored and analyzed: what's it about?
3. **Rank** — for each search, the index is sorted by relevance + quality signals.

Your job in SEO: make steps 2 and 3 easy. Say clearly what the page is about, and be genuinely useful — modern ranking is ruthless about quality.

### 4.2 Keywords: match your words to their words
A **keyword** is the phrase a person types. You might say "affordable garden office," but they search **"garden office pod"**. SEO starts by discovering the *actual* phrases — then using them naturally in your content. One page = one primary keyword (plus its natural variants). Trying to rank one page for ten topics means ranking for none.

### 4.3 Search intent beats everything
The same words mean different goals:
- **Informational:** "how to grow tomatoes" → wants a guide.
- **Navigational:** "youtube login" → wants that site.
- **Transactional:** "buy garden office pod" → wants a shop/product.

Google's front page *shows you the intent*. If every result is a listicle and yours is a product page, you won't rank — not because of tricks, but because you're answering a different question. Always search your keyword first and *look* at what's winning.

### 4.4 On-page SEO: the handful that matters
- **Title tag:** the clickable blue link. Primary keyword near the front, under ~60 characters.
- **Meta description:** the gray snippet. Not a ranking factor directly, but it's your ad copy — write it to earn the click.
- **H1 and headings:** one H1 (the page's promise); H2/H3 as a logical outline.
- **URL:** short, readable (`/garden-office-pods`, not `/p?id=8812`).
- **Content:** genuinely the best answer you can make — comprehensive but not padded.

### 4.5 A word about patience
SEO is a slow compounding asset. New pages typically take weeks to months to rank. Anyone promising "page 1 in a week" is selling something. The compounding is exactly why starting early matters.

## 5. Hands-on exercise

**Step 1 — Watch the SERP like an SEO (10 min).** Search `"best soil for tomatoes"` (or any how-to topic you know). Note: what *types* of results win (guides? videos? shopping?)? Are there "People also ask" boxes? This is search intent — Google telling you what it believes searchers want.

**Step 2 — Find real keywords for free (15 min).** Use Google itself:
1. Type a seed topic ("grow tomatoes") and screenshot/copy the **autocomplete** suggestions — these are real searches, sorted roughly by volume.
2. Scroll to **"People also ask"** — copy 3 questions.
3. Scroll to **"Related searches"** at the bottom — copy 3 more.
4. Paste them into a list. Mark each as informational (how/what/why) or transactional (buy/price/best...near me).

You've just done keyword research with zero tools. Congratulations — that's genuinely 80% of the practice.

**Step 3 — Check the competition honestly (10 min).** Pick your most promising keyword. Search it. Ask: are the first results from huge brands, or small focused sites/forums? If big brands dominate head terms like "tomatoes," go **more specific** — "growing cherry tomatoes in pots" — where small sites win. Long, specific phrases (**long-tail keywords**) have less traffic but far less competition and clearer intent. Beginners live on the long tail.

**Step 4 — Optimize a real page (15 min).** Take a page you control (or draft one in a doc). For keyword "growing cherry tomatoes in pots":
- **Title:** ≤60 chars, keyword near the front: `Growing Cherry Tomatoes in Pots: A Beginner's Guide`
- **Meta description:** ~150 chars, includes the keyword *and* a reason to click: `Learn how to grow cherry tomatoes in pots — the right container, soil, watering, and sunlight for a real harvest.`
- **H1:** matches the promise; **H2s** follow a logical order (choose pot → soil → plant → care → harvest).
- **URL:** `yoursite.com/growing-cherry-tomatoes-in-pots`

**Step 5 — Grade yourself (5 min).** Reread your page as a skeptical searcher who typed that keyword. Does the page answer, fast, fully, without fluff? If you'd bounce, so will they — and Google watches.

**Expected result:** a researched keyword list with intent labels, one chosen long-tail keyword, and an optimized title/description/headings for a real page.

## 7. Common beginner mistakes

1. **Chasing head terms.** "Tomatoes" is unwinnable for a new site. Go long-tail, win, expand.
2. **Keyword stuffing.** Repeating the keyword unnaturally. Write for the reader; use the phrase in title, H1, and a few times naturally — that's all.
3. **Ignoring intent.** A product page can't win an informational search. Match the format Google's front page already rewards.
4. **One page, ten topics.** One primary keyword per page. Ten topics = ten pages.
5. **Impatience.** Weeks-to-months is normal. Publish, optimize, move to the next page — the compounding comes.

1. Name the three jobs of a search engine, in order.
2. What's a long-tail keyword and why do beginners start there?
3. Your keyword's SERP is all listicles and you have a product page. What does this tell you?
4. What are the title tag rules (length, keyword position, job)?
5. Why isn't the meta description a direct ranking factor, and why write it carefully anyway?

1. Crawl, index, rank.
2. A longer, specific phrase with lower competition and clearer intent — realistic wins for new sites.
3. Your format doesn't match the intent; either change the page type or pick a keyword whose intent matches your page.
