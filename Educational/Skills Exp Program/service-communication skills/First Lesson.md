# First Lesson — Service Communication

> **Welcome!** Service communication is the craft of writing to customers and users — replies, updates, apologies, and explanations that leave people feeling *helped*, not handled. Whether you're in support, freelancing, or building a product, these messages are the moment your quality becomes real to people. Today you'll learn a three-part structure, rewrite a real bad reply, and build your first reply template. This is writing work: pen optional, empathy mandatory.

---

## 1. Why learn this?

- Support and customer communication is where trust is won or lost — people forgive product flaws far more readily than they forgive feeling dismissed.
- It's a skill with immediate payoff: better replies today, better reviews/relationships tomorrow.
- It compounds with everything else here: the [product creator](../product%20creator/First%20Lesson.md) answers pre-sale questions; the [product-marketing skills](../product-marketing%20skills/First%20Lesson.md) learn the audience's own words here first.

## 4. Core concepts

### 4.1 Customers read feelings first, facts second
Before they parse your solution, they ask: *does this person get it? do they care?* A technically correct reply that feels cold reads as rejection. Tone is not decoration — it's the first message your words send.

### 4.2 The AER structure
Nearly every service reply fits three beats:
1. **Acknowledge** — show you understood the specific problem and its impact. ("A week of photos you can't access — that's genuinely frustrating.")
2. **Explain** — what happened, in plain words. No internal jargon, no blame-shifting ("the system" / "as per our policy" are trust-killers).
3. **Resolve** — what happens next, concretely. Who does what by when. If you can't fix it, say what you *can* do and what you've escalated.

Order matters: people can't hear solutions until they feel heard. Acknowledge before you explain.

### 4.3 Plain language beats professional language
"Your request has been processed pursuant to our policy" says nothing. "I've issued your refund — it arrives within 3 business days" says everything. Short sentences. Everyday words. If a sentence needs rereading, it fails.

### 4.4 Ownership language
Avoid "you" accusations ("you clicked the wrong button") and avoid deflection ("that's handled by another team"). Use **"I"** and **"we"**: "I'll check this now," "we should have caught this." Ownership costs nothing and converts anger into cooperation.

### 4.5 Empathy ≠ apologizing for everything
Empathy is *acknowledging the specific impact*, not groveling. Over-apologizing reads as scripted and sometimes as insincere. One specific acknowledgment beats three generic "sorry for the inconvenience."

## 5. Hands-on exercise

**Step 1 — Read the bad reply (5 min).** A customer writes:

> "I bought your course last Tuesday, downloaded the videos, and now nothing plays. I have an interview prep session tomorrow and I paid for this. This is ridiculous."

The company's actual reply:

> "Dear customer, your ticket has been received and assigned reference #45721. Our system indicates the download was successful. Please clear your cache and try again. If the issue persists, submit a new ticket. Thank you for your patience and understanding."

**Step 2 — Diagnose it (10 min).** List everything wrong, using AER:
- Acknowledge? It never once references her interview tomorrow or the frustration — zero specific empathy.
- Explain? "Our system indicates the download was successful" blames the customer and hides behind system-speak.
- Resolve? "Try again / new ticket" — no owner, no timeline, no next step for her *tomorrow* deadline.

Also: "Dear customer" (she has a name), "thank you for your patience" (she hasn't been given anything to be patient *about*).

**Step 3 — Rewrite it (15 min).** Write your own reply, AER, plain language, ownership voice. Aim for under 120 words. A strong version looks like:

> "Hi Sarah — a broken download the day before your interview is the last thing you need, and I'm sorry. I checked your account: the file on our server was corrupted during upload, so it wasn't anything you did. I've fixed the file and re-sent the download link to your email just now — it should play fine. If anything is off before tomorrow, reply here directly and I'll jump on it personally. Good luck Thursday — you've got this."

Notice what it does: names her, names the impact, explains honestly (and takes blame off her), gives a concrete action + deadline, and opens a direct line. Feel free to steal every move.

**Step 4 — Build your template library (15 min).** Write your own 60–90 word templates for three everyday situations:
1. **Delay:** you can't deliver today. (Acknowledge the wait → honest reason → new date → what you're doing meanwhile.)
2. **No (with alternative):** you can't do what they asked. (Acknowledge the need → clear no → what you *can* do → offer.)
3. **Follow-up:** checking back after silence. (One-line context → specific question → easy out: "if now's not the time, no worries.")

Store them in a doc or a [Notion](../Notion/First%20Lesson.md) database (Name, Situation, Template, Last updated). Templates are where consistency comes from — personalization is the 20% you add each time.

**Expected result:** a diagnosed bad reply, your own strong rewrite, and three starter templates in a library.

## 6. Cheat sheet

| Principle | In practice |
|---|---|
| AER | Acknowledge → Explain → Resolve, in that order |
| Name | use their name; never "Dear customer" |
| Specific empathy | reference *their* impact, not "the inconvenience" |
| Plain language | short sentences, everyday words |
| Ownership | "I'll check now" — not "that's another team" |
| Blame | never the customer, never "the system" |
| Next step | who does what by when — always present |
| No | acknowledge → clear no → alternative |
| Over-apology | one specific acknowledgment beats three sorries |

---

## 7. Common beginner mistakes

1. **Solving before acknowledging.** The fix lands flat if they don't feel heard — AER's order is the point.
2. **Policy-speak.** "As per our policy" is how companies say "we don't care." Translate policies into human reasons.
3. **Blaming the customer.** Even when they clicked the wrong thing, the phrasing is "let's make sure this works" — never "you did it wrong."
4. **Vague timelines.** "Soon" and "shortly" create follow-up tickets. Give dates, or give honest ranges ("within 2 business days").
5. **Robotic over-apology.** Scripted sorries read as scripts. Name the specific impact instead.

## 8. Check yourself

1. Name the three AER beats and why the order matters.
2. Rewrite in plain language: "Pursuant to our terms, refunds are processed in a timely manner."
3. The customer caused the problem themselves. What do you say?
4. Why is "Dear customer" a red flag?
5. What's the difference between empathy and over-apology?

<details><summary><strong>Answers</strong></summary>

1. Acknowledge (show you understood the specific impact) → Explain (honest cause, plain words) → Resolve (who does what by when). Solutions can't land until people feel heard.
2. E.g.: "I've refunded your order — you'll see it within 3 business days."
3. Skip blame entirely; solve it: "Let's get this fixed — here's how we'll make it right," and quietly fix the underlying confusion for future customers.
4. It signals a template that wasn't personalized — the opposite of feeling heard.
5. Empathy names the customer's specific situation; over-apology repeats generic sorries that read as scripted and insincere.
