# First Lesson — Product Creator

> **Welcome!** A product creator turns knowledge, skills, or taste into something people can buy — an ebook, a course, a template pack, a tool, a service. Today you'll learn how product ideas actually work (spoiler: they're found, not invented), pick your first product candidate, and outline a *minimum version* you could realistically create. This session is thinking work — the goal is a product plan you believe in by the end.

---

## 1. Why learn this?

- Digital products have near-zero marginal cost: build once, sell while you sleep. It's the classic leverage play.
- Every skill in this collection — [Python](../Python/First%20Lesson.md), [Notion](../Notion/First%20Lesson.md) templates, [SEO](../SEO/First%20Lesson.md) content — can *become* a product. Creator is the skill that packages them.
- You don't need permission, funding, or a team to start. You need a problem, a solution, and ten people willing to pay.

**Where the other lessons meet this one:** [product-marketing skills](../product-marketing%20skills/First%20Lesson.md) is the selling half of this coin; [Content Creation](../Content%20Creation/First%20Lesson.md) builds the audience that makes launches work.

## 4. Core concepts

### 4.1 Digital products are packaged help
People don't buy ebooks or courses — they buy **outcomes**: save time, make money, look competent, feel calm. The format (PDF, video, template) is just the container. So product design starts with one question: *whose problem, and what outcome?*

### 4.2 The three wells of ideas
1. **Your own solved problems.** Anything you figured out the hard way, someone a few steps behind you needs. (You're taking this collection — a "First Lesson" for [Terminal](../Terminal/First%20Lesson.md) is a product someone would pay for.)
2. **Questions you keep answering.** In your email, DMs, comments, at work. Repeated questions = unmet demand.
3. **Your unfair advantages.** Your industry, language, niche, or past career. "Excel for kindergarten teachers" beats "Learn Excel" with zero competition.

### 4.3 Validate before you build
**Validation** = evidence that people want this *before* you spend weeks making it. The cheap ladder, from least to most expensive: (1) ask people in communities/DMs, (2) a landing page describing the product with an email signup, (3) pre-sell it ("buy now, delivers in 3 weeks"), (4) only then build. Rule: the earlier the evidence, the cheaper the mistake.

### 4.4 The MVP is the minimum *that delivers the outcome*
MVP (minimum viable product) isn't "a tiny crappy product" — it's the smallest version that still delivers the full outcome. A 10-page PDF that truly solves one narrow problem beats a 200-page one that half-solves five. **One problem, one audience, one promise.** You can expand after the first sale — never before the first.

### 4.5 Pricing: anchor on outcome, not page count
Beginners underprice wildly. A template that saves a business owner 4 hours monthly is worth more than $9 — but a $9 product is a fine *first* product: easy yes, fast learning. First product = first feedback. Price to get the learning, not to get rich.

## 5. Hands-on exercise

**Step 1 — Inventory your knowledge (15 min).** Set a timer and list 15 things you know how to do that others ask you about or that took you real effort to learn. Include the mundane ("setting up [WSL](../WSL/First%20Lesson.md) without errors"), the professional, and the hobby. Don't judge while listing — volume first, filter second.

**Step 2 — Choose a narrow audience (10 min).** For your top 2–3 items, complete this sentence precisely: *"This is for ___ who want ___."* Not "freelancers" — "new freelance designers who want their first 3 clients." Narrow feels scary; narrow is why people buy. Big audiences have bigger competition and vaguer promises.

**Step 3 — Run the filter (10 min).** Score each candidate 1–5 on:
1. **Problem pain:** how badly do they want this outcome?
2. **Your credibility:** why would they believe *you*?
3. **Reach:** do you know where these people gather (a subreddit, a community, a hashtag)?
4. **Effort:** can you ship the minimum in ≤4 weeks part-time?

Keep the highest scorer. If it fails the reach test (3), park it and pick the next — distribution first is the lesson every creator learns late.

**Step 4 — Validate cheaply this week (10 min).** Write down your validation plan: which 5 people will you ask this week (real names), which 1–2 communities will you search/post in, and what exact question you'll ask ("Would you pay $9 for a guide that does X? What's missing?"). Do not build anything yet.

**Step 5 — Outline the minimum version (15 min).** For your winner, write a one-page outline:
- **Outcome promise:** "By the end, you can ___"
- **Format:** PDF guide / 60-min video / Notion template / spreadsheet tool (choose one!)
- **Sections:** 5–7 numbered sections, each = one step toward the promise
- **The one bonus:** one checklist or template inside that makes it instantly actionable

**Expected result:** a knowledge inventory, a narrowed audience sentence, a scored candidate, a validation plan with real names, and a one-page MVP outline.

## 6. Cheat sheet

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Digital product | packaged help: ebook, course, template, tool, preset pack |
| Outcome | what the buyer can *do* after — the actual thing being sold |
| The three wells | solved problems / repeated questions / unfair advantages |
| Audience sentence | "For ___ who want ___" |
| Validation | cheap evidence of demand before building |
| Validation ladder | ask → landing page → pre-sell → build |
| MVP | smallest version that still delivers the full outcome |
| Scope creep | adding "one more thing" before launch — the killer |
| First product | priced to learn (e.g., $9–29), not to retire |

## 7. Common beginner mistakes

1. **Building before validating.** Weeks of work based on a hunch. Ask first; the ladder exists for a reason.
2. **Broad audiences.** "Everyone" is no one. The narrower the audience, the sharper the promise, the easier the sale.
3. **Multi-format paralysis.** Course + ebook + community + app for product one. One format. Ship. Expand later.
4. **Pricing by length.** Buyers pay for outcomes and time saved, not word counts — but do keep the first price easy.
5. **Waiting for the perfect idea.** Ideas are cheap and found in the wells; execution and validation are the scarce things. Your second product will be better anyway.

## 8. Check yourself

1. What do customers actually buy when they buy a digital product?
2. Name the three wells of product ideas.
3. Put the validation ladder in order, and say why pre-selling beats building first.
4. What does MVP mean here — and what does it *not* mean?
5. Your candidate scores 5-4-2-4 (pain, credibility, reach, effort). What's the problem and the fix?

<details><summary><strong>Answers</strong></summary>

1. Outcomes — saved time, money, competence, calm. The format is just the container.
2. Your own solved problems; questions you keep answering; your unfair advantages.
3. Ask → landing page → pre-sell → build. Pre-selling converts real money into the strongest evidence, before weeks of build cost.
4. The smallest version that still delivers the full outcome. Not "a sloppy full version" and not "half an outcome."
5. Reach is weak (2/5) — you don't know where the audience gathers. Park it for another product or find the community first; distribution beats idea quality at the start.
