# First Lesson — Product Marketing

> **Welcome!** Product marketing is making the right people *want* what you've built — finding them, speaking their language, and giving them a reason to act now. It's not shouting louder; it's matching words to minds. Today you'll define a target audience, position a product in one sharp sentence, and write your first landing-page headline and pitch. This session is writing work — the goal is marketing copy you'd actually ship.

---

## 1. Why learn this?

- The best product with unclear messaging loses to a decent product with clear messaging. Every time. Positioning is leverage.
- These skills apply instantly to anything you make — a product, a portfolio, a resume, even this skill collection.
- It's nearly free to learn: the tools are words, questions, and other people's homepages.

**Where the other lessons meet this one:** [product creator](../product%20creator/First%20Lesson.md) builds the thing; this makes it sell. [Content Creation](../Content%20Creation/First%20Lesson.md) earns attention; this converts it. [SEO](../SEO/First%20Lesson.md) brings the right visitors; this gives them a reason to stay.

## 4. Core concepts

### 4.1 Marketing is a conversation, not a megaphone
The megaphone view ("look at me!") is why people hate marketing. The useful view: marketing is **helping the right people understand, quickly, that you solve their problem**. Clarity converts; cleverness decorates. When you're stuck, "clear" beats "creative" every single time.

### 4.2 Audience: one person, vividly
"Women 25–40" is a statistic; **"Maya, 29, learned Python from a YouTube playlist, freezes when a tutorial says 'open a terminal'"** is a customer. The second one writes your copy for you — you know her fears, her words, her alternatives. Build this picture from real people: interviews, Reddit threads, reviews of competing products. Their *exact phrases* are your future headlines.

### 4.3 Positioning: the sentence that does the heavy lifting
Positioning states how you help, for whom, differently:

> **"___ helps ___ achieve ___ without ___."**

Example: *"KeyCoach helps beginner programmers type code fast without looking at the keyboard."* Every piece of copy you write afterward hangs off this sentence. If you can't fill the blanks convincingly, the problem is upstream — the product or the audience — not the copy.

### 4.4 Benefits vs. features (the oldest lesson, still #1)
- **Feature:** what it *is* — "120 typed drills," "10 hours of video," "200-cell dashboard."
- **Benefit:** what they *get* — "code without looking down," "ship your first automation tonight," "stop losing track of projects."

People buy outcomes. A quick test: after stating a benefit, ask "so what?" until you hit something the customer actually feels. "200-cell dashboard → so what? → you see your money situation in one glance → that's the benefit."

### 4.5 The landing page's five jobs
A simple page needs only: (1) **headline** — the promise; (2) **subheadline** — for whom/how; (3) **3 benefits** — outcomes, not features; (4) **one call-to-action (CTA)** — "Get the guide — $9"; (5) **one proof element** — a testimonial, a screenshot, a number. Five elements, one job each. Everything else is decoration (see [Web Design](../Web%20Design/First%20Lesson.md)).

## 5. Hands-on exercise

**Step 1 — Pick your subject (2 min).** Choose a real or imagined product. Struggling? Productize something from this collection: "a Notion template for tracking reading," "a guide to your first [n8n](../n8n/First%20Lesson.md) workflow," "a terminal crash course for artists."

**Step 2 — Write the audience card (13 min).** Create a half-page profile:
- **Who:** name, role, situation ("Maya, career-switcher learning to code")
- **Struggle:** what's frustrating right now, in *their* words ("I lose 20 minutes daily to typos and tab-switching")
- **Dream outcome:** what life looks like after ("I type thoughts as fast as I think them")
- **Objection:** why they'd hesitate ("I've tried and quit twice")
- **Watering holes:** where they gather (subreddits, Discords, newsletters)

**Step 3 — Write the positioning sentence (10 min).** Fill the formula: *"___ helps ___ achieve ___ without ___."* Draft three versions. Pick the one where **every word earns its place**. Read it aloud — if you stumble, simplify.

**Step 4 — Write the page (25 min).** Using the five-jobs skeleton:
- **Headline:** the outcome, concrete. Formula: *Achieve [outcome] without [pain].* Example: "Type code fast — without looking at the keyboard."
- **Subheadline:** for whom + how: "A 15-minute daily drill system for new programmers."
- **3 benefits:** outcome-first bullets ("Stop hunting for keys — build the reflex in week one").
- **CTA:** one button, one action: "Get the guide — $9."
- **Proof:** one screenshot, one honest number, or one testimonial from your Step-2 research.

**Step 5 — The stranger test (10 min).** Send your headline + subheadline + 3 benefits to one person *who resembles the audience card*. Ask exactly: *"What do you think this is, and would you click?"* Their confusion is gold — revise once and note what you changed. Confusion before launch costs nothing; after launch it costs everything.

**Expected result:** an audience card, a positioning sentence, and a five-element landing page you've tested on a real human.

## 6. Cheat sheet

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Positioning | how you help, for whom, differently — one sentence |
| Audience card | one vivid person: struggle, dream, objection, watering holes |
| Feature vs. benefit | what it is vs. what they get — sell the second |
| "So what?" test | keep asking until you hit a felt outcome |
| Headline formula | *Achieve [outcome] without [pain]* |
| CTA | one page, one primary action |
| Proof | testimonial, screenshot, or number — one is enough |
| Voice-of-customer | the audience's exact words — mine them, reuse them |
| Watering holes | where your audience gathers (subreddits, Discords) |

---

## 7. Common beginner mistakes

1. **Marketing to everyone.** "For everyone" copy persuades no one. The audience card exists to prevent exactly this.
2. **Leading with features.** "10 hours of video" is about *you*; "ship your first automation tonight" is about *them*.
3. **Clever over clear.** Puns and mystery headlines lose to plain outcome statements. Clarity is the skill.
4. **Inventing your own vocabulary.** Use the audience's words (from threads, reviews, interviews), not your internal jargon.
5. **Skipping the stranger test.** You cannot proofread your own clarity — you know too much. One honest outsider catches what hours of self-review can't.

## 8. Check yourself

1. What's the difference between a feature and a benefit — and which do you sell?
2. Write the positioning formula from memory.
3. Your audience is "developers aged 25–40." Why is that insufficient?
4. What are the five jobs of a simple landing page?
5. What is the stranger test and why can't you do it alone?

<details><summary><strong>Answers</strong></summary>

1. Feature = what it is; benefit = what they get. Sell benefits — that's what people buy.
2. *"___ helps ___ achieve ___ without ___."*
3. It's a demographic, not a person — no struggle, no words, no watering holes. You can't write copy for a statistic.
4. Headline, subheadline, 3 benefits, one CTA, one proof element.
5. Sending copy to someone resembling your audience and asking what they think it is — you can't evaluate your own clarity because you already know what it means.
</details>
